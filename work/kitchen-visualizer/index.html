Case Study Overview

A tool that answers the one question standing between a full cart and a paid order.

The challenge

An online brand sells made-to-measure cabinet doors that can't be returned. Shoppers were building complete, priced orders and then stopping at payment. Only 1 in 3 carts reached checkout.

What I did

I led the design of a Kitchen Visualizer that shows a customer's exact order on a photo of their own kitchen. I shaped the strategy, designed the experience, and built a working front-end prototype that our developer connected to the store's checkout.

Where it is now

Live for every customer who creates a cart. Using funnel data, I designed two more ways in, on product pages and inside the order form, and recommended an A/B test to find where it helps most.

The Problem

A kitchen order isn't one product. It's dozens.

Customers keep their cabinet boxes and replace the doors. Every door and drawer front is ordered to its exact size, with options like hinge holes and corner cabinets.

A typical order runs to dozens of lines and thousands of dollars. Because each piece is made for one kitchen, nothing can be returned. That makes the moment of paying feel very final.

This Wasn't a Choice Problem. It Was a Confidence Problem.

The question every hesitant shopper was really asking: will this actually look right in my kitchen?

The brief changed, so the job changed

The visualizer was first scoped for customers confirming a quote with the sales team: a final “yes, that's right.” When the client moved it to people who had built a cart but not paid, I argued this was a different job. A hesitating buyer needs space to doubt, try something else, and still be able to buy.

The principles I designed to

  1. 01Show their real order, not a sample.
  2. 02Show a price they can trust.
  3. 03Let them change their mind without starting over.
  4. 04Never leave anyone without a preview.

The Tool: Their Order, in Their Kitchen

Customers arrive with their order already loaded. They add up to three photos and, in about 30 seconds, see their doors fitted to their own cabinets, with a slider to compare before and after.

Designed for Someone Who Isn't Sure Yet

  1. 01

    A workspace, not a wizard

    My first version was a four-step flow. But big decisions aren't linear; people go back and forth. I moved to one workspace: the kitchen in the centre, options beside it, every preview kept in a tray to compare.

  2. 02

    A price that feels final

    The tool began inside a quote system, with expiry dates and “starting from” prices. To a hesitant buyer, that says the price might change. I removed all of it. The price shown is the price paid.

  3. 03

    Explore, then buy what you explored

    Trying another colour never touches the order by accident. The header says the order is unchanged, and one clear button moves the new look into the order when the customer is ready.

  4. 04

    No dead ends

    Orders mixing two door styles were going to be blocked. I proposed previewing the main style with a short note instead, so every customer gets to see their kitchen.

Two Systems, One Order, Kept in Step at Every Click

The store holds the order, prices and checkout. The visualizer lives outside it. Previewing a cart would have been simple, but exploring means every new style or colour changes dozens of priced lines at once.

How they stay in sync

  • 01 · Store → visualizerThe order comes in. The visualizer loads the full order: style, colour, every size.
  • 02 · Store → visualizerPrices always come from the store. Each new look shows a real price, never an estimate.
  • 03 · Visualizer → storeThe chosen look goes back. At checkout, the cart is rewritten to match.

I worked with the CTO and developer to agree this model before build: one shared vocabulary (an “order”, not a “quote”), prices only from the store, and rules for edge cases. I then built a working front-end prototype on that model, which gave our developer a ready front end to connect to the store's checkout.

Live Today: Meet People at the Moment of Doubt

We launched where the data said hesitation peaks. When a customer creates a cart, they get an email inviting them to see their order in their own kitchen, with their doors already selected.

Since launch, a growing number of customers are opening the visualizer and trying it with photos of their own kitchens.

Every journey is tracked

  2. Visualizer

So every change from here is measured against the number we started with: 1 in 3.

A Great Tool Doesn't Help Anyone Who Never Finds It

The email only reaches people who already built a cart. To decide where the visualizer should appear next, I went back to the data and looked at where people actually leave.

SignalWhere people leave
7 in 10order form visits end on the first screen, which asks for contact details before showing anything visual.
About 19×more people reach product pages than the order form.
Under 5%of people who started an order had looked at a product page first. The path from product to order barely exists.
Nearly allwho start entering sizes finish the form. They're committed; the doubt that remains is about paying.

That pointed to two different moments: curious shoppers on product pages, and committed buyers at the end of the form.

A
Product page

For curious shoppers

Shoppers preview a style on their kitchen before ordering anything, then start an order with that style already picked. It meets people where the traffic is, and builds the missing bridge from product to order.

B
Order form

For committed buyers

At the end of Accessories, step 3 of 4, with every door sized and priced, customers are invited to see their order in their kitchen.

It sits directly above “Review and check out”, the point where 2 in 3 carts currently stop. Their order stays exactly as it is unless they choose to change it.

Recommendation

A/B test both against today's email-only setup, measured on how many shoppers start an order and how many carts reach checkout. Both are designed and prototyped on desktop and mobile.

Measured Against the Number We Started With: 1 in 3

What I'm tracking

  • Carts that reach checkout
  • Customers opening the visualizer
  • Photos uploaded and previews seen
  • Customers who try another look
  • Checkouts and orders from the visualizer
  • With the A/B test: orders started from product pages

What I'd do next

  • Start with the product page, where most shoppers are, and let results guide the rest.
  • Make the order form's first screen show value before asking for contact details.
  • Let customers share a preview, because kitchen decisions are rarely made alone.
What I learned

Naming the real job mattered more than any screen. Once I saw this as a confidence problem, the design followed.

And designing the experience is only half the work. The other half is making sure people find it at the moment they need it.
